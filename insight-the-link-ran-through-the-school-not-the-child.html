The Link Ran Through the School, Not the Child

A study of 283 primary-school children found that dyslexia’s association with anxiety, depression and conduct problems worked indirectly, through how connected a child felt to school as a whole.

Learning Differences · Oxford Tutors · 6 minute read

What the research found

Children with dyslexia are more likely than their peers to experience anxiety, low mood and behavioral difficulties. That pattern is well established. What has been far less clear is why. Is the distress a direct consequence of the reading difficulty itself, or does it travel through something in between? This study set out to test one candidate explanation.

The researchers recruited 283 primary-school children, 87 of whom had dyslexia, along with their caregivers (95.4% of whom were mothers). Both children and caregivers completed well-validated questionnaires measuring mental health and several distinct kinds of school-connectedness: how connected a child felt to the school as a whole, and separately to teachers, to friends and to peers. The team then built two statistical models, one using the children’s own reports and one using the caregivers’ reports, to see whether dyslexia’s link to anxiety, depression and conduct problems ran directly, or indirectly through feeling connected at school.

The headline result is a shift in where the link sits. After the researchers accounted for gender and for neurodevelopmental conditions other than dyslexia, there was no direct effect of dyslexia on children’s anxiety or depression, on caregiver-reported symptoms, or on children’s own reports of conduct problems. The association had not vanished. It had moved. Dyslexia was linked to anxiety, depression and conduct problems indirectly, through lower levels of school-connectedness.

The more specific finding is the one worth holding onto. Of the several types of connectedness measured, only one carried the association: connection to the school as a whole. Feeling connected to teachers, to friends, or to peers did not do the mediating work. A child could feel warmly towards individual teachers and friends and still, if their broader sense of belonging to the institution was low, show the raised risk. This pattern held across both the child-reported and caregiver-reported models, which is a modest point in its favor, since the two accounts came from different people answering different questionnaires.

The authors frame all of this through a neurodiversity lens. The implication is that the mental-health risk associated with dyslexia may not be an unavoidable feature of the difference itself, but something shaped by the environment a child is placed in. If low whole-school belonging is part of the chain, then belonging becomes a place to intervene.

What it means for you and your child

The most useful thing this study offers a parent is a change of address for the problem. If your child has dyslexia and is anxious, low, or acting out, the instinct is often to treat the reading difficulty as the root and everything else as its shadow. This research gently redirects that instinct. The reading difficulty and the distress were connected, in this sample, largely through how much the child felt they belonged at school. That is a more workable target than the dyslexia itself.

It also reframes what “belonging” means. The finding points not at individual relationships but at the whole-school sense of fit. Your child may genuinely like their teacher and have good friends and still feel, at a broader level, that school is not a place built for them. That feeling is worth asking about directly, and it is worth raising with a school, because it is the kind of thing a school can actually change through how it includes, groups and speaks to children who learn differently.

Two cautions keep this honest. First, this is a single cross-sectional study. It measured children at one moment, so it cannot prove that low connectedness causes the distress rather than the reverse, or that both are driven by something the study did not measure. A distressed child may withdraw from school and report feeling less connected as a result. The statistical models are consistent with connectedness being a cause, but consistent with is not the same as proven. The authors themselves call for research that follows children over time before firmer conclusions are drawn.

Second, the numbers are modest and specific. Eighty-seven children with dyslexia is a meaningful sample but not a large one, and the study was carried out with primary-school children and their mostly-mother caregivers in one research program. Findings from one group do not automatically transfer to every age, setting or family. Treat this as one good piece of evidence pointing in a sensible direction, not as the final word.

What you can reasonably take from it is a question to carry into conversations with your child’s school. Not only “how is the reading coming along,” but “does my child feel they belong here.” The study suggests those are related concerns, and that the second may matter for a child’s wellbeing more than we tend to assume. Building that whole-school sense of belonging is not a substitute for good reading support. It is a companion to it, and on this evidence, a worthwhile one.
