Skipping Grades Left Their Social Selves Intact

A Dutch study compared 148 accelerated gifted students with 55 who were not. The differences in their social and emotional lives were minimal, and where they appeared, they slightly favored the accelerated group.

Giftedness · Oxford Tutors · 5 minute read

What the research found

When a gifted child skips a grade, the academic logic is easy to follow. The harder question is a human one: what happens to the child's sense of self, and to their friendships, when they are suddenly the youngest in the room? This study, conducted in the Netherlands, set out to look at exactly those social and emotional characteristics, and to compare children who had been accelerated with gifted children who had not.

The researchers compared 148 accelerated gifted students with 55 non-accelerated gifted students. The participants ranged widely in age, from 4 to 27 years, with an average of about 11 (mean 11.22, standard deviation 4.27). Rather than lean on a single questionnaire, the study gathered evidence from several angles: children reported on their own self-concept and social contacts, kept diary entries, and had their behavior rated by their parents. The researchers also looked at a range of personal and environmental factors, including gender, birth order, grade level, classroom setting, and the quality of contact between parents and school.

The headline finding was one of reassuring smallness. The differences in social-emotional characteristics between accelerated and non-accelerated gifted students were minimal. To the extent that any difference emerged, it slightly favored the accelerated students. Skipping ahead, in other words, was not associated with a diminished social or emotional life; if anything, the accelerated children looked marginally better off.

The study also spoke to a specific fear, the worry attached to multiple grade skips. Here too, the authors report no adverse effects, and they characterise the long-term effects of acceleration as tending to be positive. As for the surrounding factors they examined, the environmental and personal variables mattered mainly in the non-accelerated group, suggesting these influences played a limited role in shaping how the accelerated children fared.

It is worth being precise about what this design can and cannot show. This is a comparative, observational study, not an experiment. Children were not randomly assigned to be accelerated; families and schools made that choice, and the children chosen for acceleration may have differed at the outset in ways that are hard to fully disentangle from acceleration itself. The two groups were also quite unequal in size, and the very wide age range, from young children to adults, means the sample is a mixed one. The findings describe a Dutch educational context and a particular set of gifted students.

What it means for you and your child

The fear that stops many families from accelerating a child is not academic. It is the image of a small figure at the edge of an older group, left out of the conversation. This study addresses that image with evidence, and the evidence is calming. Across several ways of measuring it, the accelerated children's social and emotional lives looked much like those of gifted peers who stayed with their age group, and in the small differences that did appear, the accelerated children came out slightly ahead.

The point about multiple grade skips is worth dwelling on, because it is often the sharpest version of the worry. A single acceleration feels manageable; two or three feels like tempting fate. Here, even repeated acceleration was not linked to harm. That does not make it right for every child, but it does take some of the fear out of the conversation.

We would still urge you to treat this as evidence about a group, not a verdict about your child. The differences were small on average, which means both good news and a caution: acceleration did not tend to hurt these children, but averages can hide the individual who does struggle. The decision to accelerate is always a decision about one particular child, in one particular school, with one particular set of friendships. What this study offers is not a green light so much as a lowering of the alarm, so that the real questions, about readiness and fit, can be weighed without the loudest fear drowning them out.

We would offer the same caution we always do about a single study, however thoughtful. This is one comparison, in one country, of two groups that were not randomly formed. It fits, though, into a larger and fairly consistent literature suggesting that well-considered acceleration does not carry the social and emotional costs many people assume. On its own, it is a reason to ask the practical questions calmly, not a reason to accelerate any particular child.

British Journal of Educational Psychology, 82(Pt 4), 585-605. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23025394/ This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
