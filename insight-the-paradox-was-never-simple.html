The Paradox Was Never Simple

A French study of 132 children asked whether gifted children are more anxious because they are more perfectionist. The answer was more careful than the question.

Giftedness · Oxford Tutors · 6 minute read

What the research found

There is a long-standing belief that gifted children carry more anxiety than their peers, and that perfectionism is the reason. A 2012 study by Guignard, Jacquet and Lubart, published in PLoS One, set out to test that belief directly. It is a modest, careful piece of work, and its main contribution is to complicate a story that is usually told too simply.

The researchers studied 132 children in the suburbs of Paris. Sixty-one were gifted sixth-graders, identified as having an IQ above 130 on the Wechsler scales or Cattell’s Culture Fair Test. Fifty-one were non-gifted sixth-graders. A further 20 were non-gifted fifth-graders, included as a younger comparison group of a similar age to the gifted children. Every child completed two questionnaires: the Child-Adolescent Perfectionism Scale and the Revised Children’s Manifest Anxiety Scale. This is a cross-sectional design, which means the children were measured once, at a single moment, rather than followed over time.

Perfectionism was split into two kinds. Self-oriented perfectionism is the pressure a child puts on themselves to reach very high standards. Socially-prescribed perfectionism is the pressure a child feels coming from other people. Across the whole sample, perfectionism and anxiety were positively linked (r = .35, p < .01). A correlation of that size is real but moderate: the two travel together, but knowing one tells you only a little about the other.

The gifted sixth-graders did score higher on self-oriented perfectionism than the non-gifted sixth-graders (mean 38.05 versus 33.90; t = 2.37, p < .05). If perfectionism straightforwardly drove anxiety, you would then expect the gifted children to be the most anxious group. They were not. Compared with the non-gifted sixth-graders, the gifted children showed no significant difference in anxiety at all, despite their higher perfectionism.

The puzzle deepened with the younger group. The gifted sixth-graders reported more anxiety than the non-gifted fifth-graders even though the two groups had similar perfectionism levels. So higher perfectionism did not always bring higher anxiety, and higher anxiety did not always come with higher perfectionism. This is the paradox in the paper’s title. The authors conclude that the link between perfectionism and anxiety in giftedness is “more complex than a simple linear relationship” and depends on developmental context rather than on giftedness by itself.

What it means for you and your child

The plain takeaway is reassuring, and it is worth stating clearly. This study did not find that gifted children are more anxious than their classmates. The gifted sixth-graders were more demanding of themselves, but that extra self-pressure did not translate into more anxiety than their same-age peers carried. If you have quietly worried that your child’s high standards are a hidden source of distress, this evidence does not support that fear. Perfectionism and anxiety are related, but they are not the same thing, and one does not simply produce the other.

It also means the familiar image of the fragile, over-anxious gifted child is not well supported here. High personal standards can coexist with ordinary levels of anxiety. Many gifted children set themselves demanding goals and manage them without strain. The presence of perfectionism in your child is not, on its own, a warning sign. What matters is whether the standards feel motivating or crushing, and that is something you can observe day to day rather than infer from a label.

Be careful, though, about how far a study like this can carry you. It was measured at one point in time, so it cannot tell us what causes what, or how these children fare a year or five years later. The comparison group of younger children was small, only 20 pupils, which makes those particular contrasts less certain. And the gifted children were all already identified and placed in special programs. Children whose giftedness goes unrecognised may have a different experience entirely, and the authors say so plainly. This is one snapshot of one group of French schoolchildren, not the last word.

The more useful signal for a parent is the distinction the study draws within perfectionism itself. Self-oriented perfectionism, the standards a child sets for themselves, behaves differently from socially-prescribed perfectionism, the pressure a child feels from others. It is worth noticing which kind you may be feeding. Praise aimed at effort and curiosity tends to support the first; anxiety about grades, comparisons and other people’s expectations tends to feed the second, which is the strand more consistently tied to social stress.

None of this replaces attention to the individual child in front of you. A single correlation of .35 describes a group; it says nothing certain about any one pupil. If your child shows genuine signs of anxiety, sleeplessness, avoidance, distress over small mistakes, that deserves a direct response regardless of what the averages say. The value of this research is quieter. It gently unpicks a myth, and reminds us that giftedness and anxiety are not bound together as tightly as folklore suggests.

Drawn from: Guignard J-H, Jacquet A-Y, Lubart TI. Perfectionism and Anxiety: A Paradox in Intellectual Giftedness? PLoS One. 2012;7(7):e41043. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0041043 This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
