A Review, Not a Verdict, on Gifted Children’s Emotional Lives

A 2021 narrative review gathered sixteen studies on how parenting shapes the social and emotional lives of gifted children. It found consistent patterns, but no proof of cause.

Giftedness · Oxford Tutors · 6 minute read

What the research found

In 2021, Dimitrios Papadopoulos of the University of Crete published a review in the journal Children titled “Parenting the Exceptional Social-Emotional Needs of Gifted and Talented Children: What Do We Know?” It is worth being precise about what kind of paper this is. It is not a new experiment. It is a narrative review: a single author reading the existing literature and reporting what it collectively suggests. He identified sixteen peer-reviewed studies published between 2000 and 2020 that met his criteria, and drew his conclusions from those.

That distinction matters because a review inherits the strengths and weaknesses of the studies inside it. Some of those studies were substantial. One cited work, Campbell and Verna (2007), drew on 2,866 parents and 10,062 students across Europe, Asia and the United States. Another, Olszewski-Kubilius and colleagues (2014), examined 1,526 parents and children in the United States and South Korea. A smaller, frequently referenced study by Morawska and Sanders involved 211 parents of gifted children. These are the kinds of numbers a parent should keep in mind: they describe patterns across large groups, not a rule about any one family.

Three findings recur across the review. The first concerns parenting style. Studies consistently associated an authoritative style, meaning warm but with clear expectations, with better mental health in gifted adolescents, while a more controlling authoritarian style was linked to poorer wellbeing. The same broad pattern appears in research on children who are not gifted, so this is not unique to the gifted population, but it held here too.

The second finding is about responsiveness. Parents of gifted children were, on average, described as more engaged, caring and interactive with their children than parents of average-ability peers. The third is about the family as a whole: gifted children tended to rate their families as cohesive and flexible, with high satisfaction and open communication among members.

Read plainly, the review says that supportive, responsive, well-structured family environments tend to travel alongside better emotional outcomes for gifted children. What it cannot say is that one produces the other. Almost every study drew on parents’ own reports at a single point in time. That design shows what goes together; it cannot show what causes what.

What it means for you and your child

The honest headline is that this paper describes a correlation and is careful not to claim more. The author himself lists the limitations, and they are the ones that should shape how much weight you give it. The studies leaned heavily on parental self-report, which is vulnerable to bias, because parents tend to describe themselves favourably. Most were cross-sectional, so cause and effect cannot be separated. The samples were largely middle-class, and children from minority backgrounds were under-represented. And the studies did not even agree on how to define giftedness in the first place.

So it would be a mistake to read this and conclude that a particular parenting move will produce a particular emotional result in your child. A cohesive, communicative family and a thriving gifted child often appear together, but the arrow could point either way. A settled, secure child may make family life feel more cohesive, just as easily as a cohesive family may settle the child. Most likely both are true at once. A review of correlational studies cannot untangle that, and this one does not pretend to.

What the review can reasonably offer is direction rather than instruction. The pattern that warmth combined with clear, consistent expectations tends to accompany better wellbeing is one of the most durable findings in child development, and it is reassuring to see it hold for gifted children specifically. Nothing here suggests gifted children need an exotic or specialized parenting philosophy. They appear to benefit from the same fundamentals as other children: to be listened to, to be held to fair expectations, and to feel their family is a place of open communication.

It is also worth noting what the review does not establish. It does not show that gifted children are inherently more fragile, nor that they are uniformly resilient. It gathers evidence that their emotional lives are shaped by the same family dynamics that shape any child’s, and that responsive parenting is a common thread in the better outcomes. That is a modest, useful conclusion, and it is stated modestly.

If you take one thing from this study, let it be a sense of proportion. A single narrative review of sixteen mostly self-reported studies is a starting point for thinking, not a manual for acting. It points to the value of warmth, structure and honest communication at home. It stops well short of promising that these guarantee an emotionally settled gifted child, and you should be wary of anyone who tells you it does.

Drawn from: Papadopoulos, D. (2021). Parenting the Exceptional Social-Emotional Needs of Gifted and Talented Children: What Do We Know? Children (Basel), 8(11), 953. https://doi.org/10.3390/children8110953 This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
