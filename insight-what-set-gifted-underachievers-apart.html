What Set Gifted Underachievers Apart

A Spanish study of 164 gifted secondary students asked what separates those who underachieve from those who do not. The differences were not about ability.

Giftedness · Oxford Tutors · 5 minute read

What the research found

Among the more painful puzzles a parent can face is the gifted child who is not doing well at school. The ability is not in doubt; the results are. This study set out to describe that gap in a disciplined way, by asking what actually distinguishes gifted students who underachieve from equally able students who do not.

The researchers worked in Spain, starting from a larger pool of 1,400 secondary-school students and identifying 164 as gifted, using a threshold of an IQ of 120 or above. Within that gifted group, they separated the underachievers from the rest. Underachievement is a slippery thing to define, so the study took it seriously, using three different methods to identify it: a standardised-difference approach, a regression approach, and a Rasch approach. Only after settling who counted as an underachiever did they compare the two groups across a range of personal, familial, social, and school factors.

The picture that emerged is consistent and, in a way, clarifying. The underachieving gifted students scored significantly lower than their achieving peers on a specific cluster of characteristics. On the personal side, they were weaker in learning strategies, in goal orientations, and in academic self-concept. On the social side, they held less positive attitudes toward their teachers. And in the space where family and school meet, they reported lower parental involvement in their schooling.

What is striking is what does not appear on that list: ability. These were, by definition, gifted children on both sides of the divide. The difference between the ones who thrived and the ones who did not lay in how they approached learning, how they saw themselves as students, how they related to their teachers, and how present their families were in their school lives.

It is worth being precise about what this design can and cannot show. This is a cross-sectional study, a comparison made at a single point in time. It can tell us that underachievement travels together with weaker learning strategies, a lower academic self-concept, and less parental involvement, but it cannot tell us which came first. A child might underachieve because of a fragile self-concept, or develop a fragile self-concept because of a run of poor results. The study also reports which factors differed but not the size of each gap, and its findings belong to Spanish secondary schools.

What it means for you and your child

The most useful message here is one of reframing. When a gifted child underachieves, the instinct is often to search for a hidden deficit, some cognitive shortfall that the giftedness was masking. This study points elsewhere. The children who struggled were not less able; they differed in the machinery around the ability, the strategies, the self-belief, the relationships, the support.

That is, on balance, hopeful news, because those things are more open to change than raw ability. Learning strategies can be taught. A battered academic self-concept can, slowly, be rebuilt. Attitudes toward teachers often shift when a single relationship improves. And the finding about parental involvement is not a rebuke to busy families; it is a reminder that a gifted child's engagement with school is not purely internal, and that presence at home has a measurable partner in performance at school.

We would be careful, though, not to read the arrows too confidently. Because this is a snapshot, we cannot say that fixing any one of these factors will lift a child's results, and it would be a mistake to treat the list as a checklist that guarantees a turnaround. What the study offers is a better place to look. If a bright child is underachieving, the more productive questions may not be about their intelligence at all, but about how they study, how they see themselves, whom they trust at school, and how connected home and classroom feel.

We would offer the same caution we always do about a single study, however carefully done. This is one comparison, in one country, at one moment. It fits a wider literature that has long located underachievement in motivation and environment rather than ability, and to that literature it adds a clear, well-identified example. On its own, it is a reason to broaden where you look when a gifted child stalls, not a formula for restarting them.

Frontiers in Psychology / Anales de Psicología, 2019. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31695651/ This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
