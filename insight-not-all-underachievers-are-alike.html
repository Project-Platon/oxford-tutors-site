Not All Underachievers Are Alike

A Belgian study of 403 high-ability adolescents found more than one kind of underachiever, each with a distinct motivational profile that showed up in grades and in what parents and teachers saw.

Giftedness · Oxford Tutors · 5 minute read

What the research found

Underachievement in a capable child is usually spoken of as a single problem, as though every bright student who falls short is falling short in the same way. This study set out to test that assumption, and it found the truth to be more varied. Among high-ability adolescents, there was not one kind of struggling motivation but several.

The researchers studied 403 high-ability adolescents in Flanders, Belgium, all with an IQ of 120 or above, at an average age of about 12. Rather than sort children into simple bins of achiever and underachiever, they used a technique called latent profile analysis, which looks for naturally occurring clusters of students who share a pattern across several traits at once. Here the traits were motivational: academic self-concept, how much a student's self-worth hinged on achievement, how much they valued their schoolwork, whether they believed ability was fixed, and how useful they judged their learning to be.

Four distinct profiles emerged from the data. Two of them were healthy enough; two were what the researchers call maladaptive, matching patterns that a theoretical model of underachievement, the Pathways to Underachievement model, had predicted in advance. In other words, the study did not just find that some able students are poorly motivated; it found qualitatively different flavours of poor motivation, each with its own configuration of beliefs and values.

Crucially, these profiles were not just abstractions on a questionnaire. The differences between them lined up with real-world engagement and achievement, and they did so across several vantage points: the students' own reports, their parents' observations, their teachers' observations, and their actual school grades. When several independent witnesses and the grade book all agree, a profile is describing something real.

It is worth being precise about what this design can and cannot show. This is a cross-sectional study, a portrait at one point in time. It can identify distinct types of motivation and show that they correspond to different outcomes, but it cannot trace how a child comes to occupy one profile, or whether they move between profiles as they grow. The profiles are also statistical groupings; a given child may sit near a boundary rather than squarely inside a type. And the sample is Flemish high-ability twelve-year-olds, a specific group.

What it means for you and your child

The practical heart of this study is a warning against a single explanation. If your gifted child is underperforming, the reason is not generic. One able underachiever may be paralysed by tying their whole self-worth to results, so that any risk of failure feels unbearable. Another may simply place little value on the work in front of them. A third may believe, quietly, that ability is fixed and effort pointless. These look similar from the outside, a good mind and disappointing grades, but they are different problems, and they call for different responses.

That matters because the wrong remedy can miss entirely. Reassuring a child who over-identifies with achievement is very different from motivating a child who finds the material pointless, which is different again from challenging a child's belief that intelligence cannot grow. A generic push to try harder may help one of these children and actively harm another. The first useful question is not how do I fix underachievement, but which kind of underachievement is this.

There is reassurance, too, in the finding that the profiles showed up consistently across parents, teachers, and grades. It suggests that what you observe at home about your child's motivation is real signal, not just parental worry, and that it is worth comparing notes with teachers, who may be seeing the same pattern from another angle. Between you, a clearer picture of which pattern you are dealing with can emerge.

We would offer the same caution we always do about a single study, however elegant its method. This is one cross-sectional analysis of one group of Belgian adolescents. It shows that distinct types of high-ability underachiever exist; it does not tell you how to move a child from a struggling profile to a thriving one. Read alongside the wider literature on motivation, it supports a genuinely useful stance. On its own, it is a reason to diagnose before you prescribe, not a manual for the cure.
