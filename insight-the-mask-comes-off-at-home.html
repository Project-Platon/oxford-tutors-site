The Mask Comes Off at Home

A close interview study of eight autistic girls found that the effort of hiding autism at school often failed on its own terms, and the exhaustion it produced surfaced only once the school day was over.

Autism · Oxford Tutors · 6 minute read

What the research found

Camouflaging is the effort autistic people make to hide autistic traits and pass as non-autistic. A child might force eye contact, rehearse conversations, suppress the urge to talk about a favourite subject, or copy how other children behave. This study looked at how that effort plays out for adolescent girls, and at the price it carries.

The researchers interviewed eight autistic girls, their mothers, and their educators. That is a small number, and it is worth stating plainly. All eight girls attended a resource base: a specialist unit attached to a mainstream school, where pupils move between small specialist classes and ordinary mainstream lessons. The girls were of secondary-school age. Alongside the eight girls, eight mothers and seven school staff took part, most of them teaching assistants who worked closely with the girls day to day. The interviews were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis, a method that draws out recurring patterns of meaning across what people say. This is qualitative work. It describes experience in depth; it does not measure how common that experience is.

The first finding is that the camouflaging often did not work. The girls tried to blend into mainstream classes but were rarely able to hold on to the friendships they wanted with neurotypical peers. Yet they also resisted identifying with other pupils in the resource base. The result was a kind of no-man’s-land, described by one educator as the girls being neither ordinary enough for one group nor comfortable enough in the other. The effort of masking bought them the costs of the strategy without its rewards.

The second finding concerns exhaustion, which the participants named as the single heaviest consequence. One girl described camouflaging simply as “really tiring.” Mothers and educators described the same thing at greater length. The strain tended to become visible after the pressure eased: in the resource base after a mainstream lesson, and above all at home after school. Girls who had held themselves together all day became angry, tearful, or dysregulated once they were somewhere safe. As one girl put it, “if I’m angry at school, most of the time I try and hide it.” The calm face teachers saw was not the whole picture.

The third finding is a gap in perception. Educators generally believed the girls felt free to be themselves in the resource base and masked only in mainstream classes. The girls said otherwise: most reported camouflaging in every school setting, including the specialist one. The authors link this to the double empathy problem, the idea that misunderstanding between autistic and non-autistic people runs in both directions. The girls also worked hard to hide learning difficulties, dreading being seen to get something wrong, which sometimes trapped them in a cycle where the effort of concealment ate into the capacity to learn.

What this means for you and your child

Read carefully, this study tells you something specific and something limited. The specific part is a mechanism worth understanding: a child can appear to cope at school precisely because coping is taking enormous effort, and the cost of that effort may land at home, out of the teacher’s sight. A settled school report and a child who melts down every afternoon are not a contradiction. They can be two views of the same day.

If that pattern sounds familiar, the after-school distress is worth taking seriously as information rather than treating it as misbehaviour or a home-only problem. The girls in this study were not calmer at school than at home in any real sense. They were more guarded. The difference between the two settings was not how they felt but how much they were willing to show. A conversation with the school that shares what you see at home may help staff read signs they are not currently seeing.

Be cautious, though, about how far you carry these findings. Eight girls, all in one type of setting, cannot tell you how typical any of this is, and the authors say so directly. They list their own limitations honestly: the sample is small; there were no autistic boys or non-autistic girls to compare against, so we cannot be sure how much is specific to autistic girls; and eliciting the views of autistic children through interviews is itself difficult. They even note the possibility that the girls were camouflaging during the interviews. This is a study that maps a terrain in detail. It does not tell you the odds that your own child is on it.

It also cannot establish cause and effect. The research describes camouflaging and distress occurring together and being linked by the people involved. It does not prove that masking produces exhaustion or shapes mental health over time, because a set of interviews at one point cannot show that. What it offers is a plausible and vivid account, consistent with a wider body of work, of how the two travel together.

The practical takeaway is modest and, we think, sound. Notice the gap between the child you see at home and the child the school describes, and treat that gap as something to be curious about rather than to explain away. The authors argue that the better long-term answer is not to coach girls into masking more skilfully, but to build settings that make so much concealment unnecessary. For a parent, the first step is smaller and immediately available: to recognize that a mask held in place all day has to come off somewhere, and that home is usually where it does.

Drawn from: Halsall J, Clarke C, Crane L. “Camouflaging” by adolescent autistic girls who attend both mainstream and specialist resource classes: Perspectives of girls, their mothers and their educators. Autism. 2021;25(7):2074-2086. doi:10.1177/13623613211012819 This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
