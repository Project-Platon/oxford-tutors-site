Eleanor Whitfield

Lead Tutor · Mathematics & Sciences

A former Oxford examiner who has spent fifteen years helping students arrive at the calm, unhurried confidence that renders a formidable subject familiar.

Biography

Eleanor read Mathematics at Merton College, Oxford, before completing a Master of Philosophy in Education concerned with how adolescents form durable habits of problem-solving. She has taught in classrooms, examined at the university level, and for the past decade worked privately with students who had come to believe the subject was simply not meant for them.

Her students span the full range: those preparing for the university admissions papers in mathematics, secondary candidates rebuilding uncertain foundations, and younger learners who need someone willing to slow down and begin again from first principles. What unites them is seldom a want of ability. More often it is a gap that was never quite closed, and the quiet anxiety that gathers around it.

Eleanor works with a small number of families at any one time, so that each relationship may unfold over years rather than weeks. She lives in Boston and teaches online across time zones, with occasional in-person work throughout the Northeast.

Teaching philosophy

Confidence is not a matter of encouragement; it is the settled sense that one has met this kind of problem before and prevailed. The work therefore begins by locating the precise place where understanding first faltered, often several years earlier than anyone had supposed, and rebuilding from there, one secure step at a time.

I resist the urge to rescue. A student handed the answer learns that they required rescuing; a student given room to arrive at it learns that they are equal to the task. My part is to make that room safe enough that the effort feels worth making, and to name the moment aloud when it succeeds.

Progress that endures is quiet. It resembles a student explaining a concept back to me unprompted, or reaching for a method a month after we first encountered it. I would sooner cultivate that than pursue a single fortunate result, for the former tends to produce the latter of its own accord.
