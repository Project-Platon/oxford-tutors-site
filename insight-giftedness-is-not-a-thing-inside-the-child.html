Giftedness Is Not a Thing Inside the Child

A conceptual essay by Robert Sternberg argues that the social and emotional life of a gifted child cannot be separated from the giftedness itself. It reframes old assumptions rather than testing new ones.

Giftedness · Oxford Tutors · 7 minute read

What the research found

This is a single essay, not a study. Robert J. Sternberg, a psychologist at Cornell, wrote it alone and published it in the journal Behavioral Sciences in 2024. It has no participants, no data set, no survey and no statistical result of its own. It is an argument. That distinction matters, and we will return to it, but it is worth stating plainly at the outset: nothing here was measured. It was reasoned.

The argument takes aim at a habit of thought rather than a finding. For decades, gifted education has treated a child’s social and emotional life as a separate compartment sitting alongside the “real” gift, which is assumed to be cognitive. Sternberg calls this a mistake, and he makes three claims against it. First, that a child’s social and emotional development is not a companion to giftedness but part of it. Second, that the emotional and the cognitive cannot be cleanly pulled apart in the first place. Third, and most provocatively, that giftedness does not sit inside a person at all. On his account it is an interaction between a person, a task and a situation, and it changes when any of those three changes.

He grounds this in some familiar history. Lewis Terman, studying gifted children a century ago, already dispelled the myth that they were social misfits or “freaks.” Sternberg’s complaint is that the field never fully absorbed the lesson. It still identifies children mainly by cognitive tests and treats their inner lives as a secondary concern. He points to asynchrony, the well-known observation that a gifted child’s intellect can outrun their emotional age, and reframes it. Asynchrony, in his telling, is not a disorder to be corrected but a reality to be understood, one that leaves a child vulnerable mainly when the adults around them misread it.

Sternberg also questions how confidently we can measure any of this. He argues that the tests we use to identify gifted children are entangled with advantage. He cites a striking figure: that the squared correlation between family income and SAT scores reaches roughly 0.95. That number, if taken at face value, implies that variation in family income tracks almost perfectly with variation in scores. It is his claim within an essay, not a fresh result, and it should be read as an illustration of his point rather than an established statistic. His broader case is that advantages compound: money opens doors that open further doors, until genuine ability and inherited privilege become almost impossible to separate on a test sheet.

The rest of the essay widens the lens. He walks through social intelligence, practical intelligence and emotional intelligence, drawing on his own long research program and on others’, including cross-cultural work in rural Kenya where practical skill and school-based intelligence pulled in opposite directions. His conclusion is a plea for integration: giftedness, he writes, should be understood, identified and taught as a whole, encompassing cognitive, emotional, task and situational elements together. High test scores alone, he insists, do not tell us who will do what matters.

What it means for you and your child

The honest headline is that this paper changes how to think, not what to do. It offers no intervention, no threshold, no measured outcome you can act on. A conceptual essay can sharpen a question and expose a hidden assumption. It cannot, on its own, tell you that a particular approach helps children, because it tested nothing. Treat it as a well-argued lens, and hold its bolder claims, including that 0.95 figure, at arm’s length until you see them replicated in data.

With that caution in place, the reframing is genuinely useful for a parent. If your child is gifted, you have probably been handed the compartment model without noticing: here is the clever child, and separately, here are the emotions we manage on the side. Sternberg’s case is that the two were never separate. The intensity, the perfectionism, the sensitivity, the frustration when the world moves too slowly, these are not side effects of the intellect. They are of a piece with it. That framing can lower the temperature at home, because it stops you treating emotional struggle as a flaw bolted onto an otherwise fine machine.

The point about asynchrony is the most immediately practical. A ten-year-old who reasons like an adult and cries like a ten-year-old is not broken and is not behind. The mismatch is normal for the profile. Where it causes harm, Sternberg suggests, is usually when adults expect the emotional maturity to match the intellectual maturity and are disappointed when it does not. Adjusting your own expectations is often the intervention, and it is one you can make today without any further evidence.

His argument that giftedness lives in the interaction of person, task and situation also has a quiet, freeing implication. If a gift shows up in one setting and vanishes in another, that is not your child being inconsistent or “not really” gifted. It is what the essay predicts. The right response is to attend to the conditions, the task and the environment, rather than to hunt for a fixed quantity of talent inside the child that must be either present or absent.

Finally, keep the essay in proportion. It is one respected scholar’s reframing, offered as argument. It should make you more sceptical of any school or program that reduces your child to a single score, and more attentive to the whole child in front of you. It should not be read as proof that a specific method works. For that, we still wait on the studies this essay is asking the field to run.

Drawn from: Sternberg, R. J. (2024). Reframing Social and Emotional Development of the Gifted. Behavioral Sciences, 14(9), 752. https://doi.org/10.3390/bs14090752 (PubMed 39335968). This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
