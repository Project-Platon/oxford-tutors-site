Irony Runs Through a Second Mind

A small 2024 study finds that autistic children’s difficulty with irony tracks one specific skill: reasoning about what one person thinks another person believes.

Autism · Oxford Tutors · 6 minute read

What the research found

Irony is a small linguistic trick with a large cognitive demand. When someone stands in the rain and says “lovely weather,” the words mean the opposite of what they state. To catch this, a listener has to hold two things in mind at once: what was said, and what the speaker actually believes. A 2024 study in Autism Research set out to ask why this is harder for many autistic children, and which underlying skill the difficulty depends on.

The researchers, Yongning Song, Ziyun Nie and Jiatong Shan, compared 20 autistic children with 25 typically developing children. That is 45 children in total, a small sample, and worth keeping in view as you read what follows. Each child completed three sets of tasks: an irony task using story pictures, a theory of mind task, and an executive function task. Theory of mind is the everyday ability to reason about other people’s mental states. Executive function is the family of skills that let us plan, hold information in working memory and resist an obvious-but-wrong response.

The first result was the expected one. The autistic children understood irony less well than their typically developing peers. Performance on the ironic stories was distinctive enough that it separated the two groups with, in the authors’ words, a significant moderate discriminatory effect. In plain terms, how a child handled irony was a reasonable, though not perfect, signal of which group they were in.

The more interesting result concerns what irony understanding was tied to. Comprehension of irony correlated significantly with second-order theory of mind, the ability to reason about what one person thinks a second person believes (“she thinks that he thinks...”). It did not correlate significantly with any component of executive function. So the children’s planning and working-memory skills were not what set irony apart. The mind-reading skill was.

The authors then went one step further with a mediation analysis. This is a statistical technique that asks whether the link between two things runs through a third. Here, second-order theory of mind mediated the relationship between autism symptoms and irony comprehension. Put simply, the connection between a child’s autism symptoms and their difficulty with irony appeared to pass through this specific mind-reading skill, rather than being a direct effect. The authors suggest that irony comprehension might one day serve as a cognitive marker of how autism presents, and that second-order theory of mind may be the mechanism underneath.

What it means for you and your child

The honest headline is that this is one small, careful study, not a verdict. Forty-five children is not many, and the two groups were compared at a single point in time. A correlation, even a clean one, does not prove that weaker second-order theory of mind causes weaker irony understanding. The mediation analysis is suggestive of a pathway, but it rests on data gathered all at once, so it cannot establish that one thing produces another over time. What the study offers is a plausible and specific hypothesis, well aligned with earlier work, that deserves testing in larger and longer samples before anyone treats it as settled.

With that caution in place, the finding is genuinely useful for how you think about your child. If your autistic child stumbles over sarcasm, teasing or a dry aside, this study suggests the obstacle may not be a general processing or attention problem. It points instead to a particular reasoning step: holding in mind what one person believes about another person’s thoughts. That is a narrower and more approachable target than “trouble with language” or “trouble concentrating.”

It also reframes what irony difficulty is not. Because executive function did not track irony here, a child who struggles with sarcasm is not necessarily a child who will struggle with planning or working memory, and the reverse holds too. These are separable skills. It is worth resisting the tempting story that one weakness explains everything. A child can be quick and organized and still miss the joke, and that pattern is coherent rather than contradictory.

For everyday life, the practical move is to make the hidden layer visible. When irony appears, you can narrate the two minds involved out loud: what the speaker said, what the speaker actually thinks, and how you can tell the two apart from the situation. Second-order reasoning is exactly the kind of skill that can be modelled patiently in ordinary conversation, in stories and in gentle after-the-fact explanation. The study does not test any teaching method, so this is a reasonable extension of its logic rather than a proven intervention.

Finally, keep the marker idea in proportion. The authors raise the possibility that irony comprehension could help quantify how autism shows up in a given child. That is a research direction, not a diagnostic tool, and nothing here should be read as a test to run at home or a benchmark your child must meet. The steadier takeaway is quieter and more helpful: when the words say one thing and mean another, the work happening underneath is second-order mind-reading, and that is a skill you can understand, watch for and support.

Drawn from: Song Y, Nie Z, Shan J. Comprehension of irony in autistic children: The role of theory of mind and executive function. Autism Res. 2024;17(1):109-124. doi:10.1002/aur.3051 This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
