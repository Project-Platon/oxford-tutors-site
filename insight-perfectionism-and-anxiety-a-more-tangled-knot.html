Perfectionism and Anxiety: A More Tangled Knot

A French study of 132 children tested the assumption that gifted children are more perfectionistic and more anxious. The relationship shifted between two school years.

Giftedness · Oxford Tutors · 5 minute read

What the research found

Two ideas cling to the gifted child in the popular imagination: that they are perfectionists, and that they are anxious, and that the one feeds the other. It is an intuitive story. A child who holds themselves to impossibly high standards will, surely, worry more. This study went looking for that neat chain and found something less tidy.

The researchers studied 132 children in France, in the fifth and sixth grades. They measured two things with established instruments: perfectionism, using a children's perfectionism scale, and anxiety, using a revised children's manifest anxiety scale. The design let them compare gifted children with their classmates and, importantly, to do so separately at two grade levels, which turned out to matter a great deal.

Had the researchers looked at only one grade, they might have told a simpler and misleading story. In the sixth grade, the gifted children did show higher self-oriented perfectionism, the kind that comes from within, the standards a child sets for themselves. Yet their anxiety was no higher than their peers'. So here was perfectionism without the expected anxiety. In the fifth grade, the pattern flipped. Gifted and non-gifted children looked alike on perfectionism, but the gifted children reported significantly more anxiety. Here was anxiety without heightened perfectionism.

The authors' conclusion is a caution against the tidy version. The interplay between perfectionism and anxiety in giftedness, they write, is more complex than a simple linear relationship. It is not that more perfectionism reliably brings more anxiety. The two can appear apart, and which one shows up seems to depend on the developmental moment.

It is worth being precise about what this design can and cannot show. This is a comparison at a single point in time, not a study that followed the same children across the two grades. So while it shows the pattern differs between fifth and sixth graders, it cannot show a single child moving from one pattern to the other. The samples within each grade are modest, and self-report measures of perfectionism and anxiety in children capture what children can name, not everything they feel. The findings belong to French upper-primary pupils.

What it means for you and your child

The value of this study is that it complicates a story parents are often handed. If you have been told that your gifted child's perfectionism is the engine of their anxiety, this research suggests the machinery is not so simple. Perfectionism and anxiety are real, and both can appear in able children, but they do not march in lockstep. A perfectionistic child is not automatically an anxious one, and an anxious child is not necessarily a perfectionist.

That matters for what you do. If you assume the two are welded together, you might try to ease a child's anxiety by attacking their high standards, or dismiss their perfectionism as harmless because they seem calm. This study suggests treating them as separate things worth watching in their own right. The self-oriented perfectionism seen in the older children, standards set from within, is not in itself a warning sign; it coexisted with ordinary levels of anxiety. The anxiety seen in the younger children is worth attending to whether or not perfectionism is present.

There is also a gentle lesson about age. The pattern differed between two grades that are only a year apart, which is a reminder that a gifted child's emotional profile is not a fixed trait but something that moves with development. What is true of your child at ten may not describe them at eleven. That argues for watching and re-watching, rather than fixing a label early and carrying it forward.

We would offer the same caution we always do about a single study, however thought-provoking. This is one snapshot, of a modest number of French children, at two grade levels. Its real contribution is to unsettle an oversimplified assumption, and that is a useful thing for a single study to do. Read alongside the broader and often conflicting literature on perfectionism in gifted children, it counsels humility. On its own, it is a reason to treat perfectionism and anxiety as separate questions about your child, not a single knot to cut.

Drawn from: Guignard, J.-H., Jacquet, A.-Y., & Lubart, T. I. (2012). Perfectionism and anxiety: a paradox in intellectual giftedness? PLoS ONE, 7(7), e41043. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22859964/ This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
