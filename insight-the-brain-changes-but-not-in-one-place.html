The Brain Changes, But Not in One Place

A large review of reading-intervention studies finds that children’s brains do change after good teaching. It also finds that no two studies quite agree on where or how.

Learning Differences · Oxford Tutors · 6 minute read

What the research found

There is a hopeful idea that circulates among parents of children who struggle to read. It is that the right intervention does not merely improve a child’s reading, it rewires the brain, and that we can watch this happen on a scan. This review, published in Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews in 2022, set out to test how much of that idea is actually supported by the evidence. The answer is careful, and worth sitting with.

The authors gathered every study they could find that scanned the brains of struggling or at-risk readers before and after a reading intervention. Thirty-nine studies met their standard for the descriptive review. The participants ranged widely, from children in kindergarten to adults in their forties, with individual studies enrolling anywhere from around fourteen to seventy-two people. This is the largest and most systematic look at the question to date.

Their headline result is a quiet one. When they pooled the eight studies rigorous enough for formal statistical combination, they found nothing. In their own words, the meta-analysis of changes in brain activation from before to after intervention “did not yield any significant effects.” That does not mean nothing happened in those children’s brains. It means that across the eight studies, the brain changes did not line up in the same place often enough to register as a reliable, shared pattern.

Look at the studies one by one, and you do see change. Reading intervention shifted activity across a wide set of regions involved in recognizing and sounding out words. One finding concerned the connections between regions rather than the regions themselves: coordination between two areas on the right side of the brain increased after intervention. Another looked at white matter, the brain’s wiring. In a tract on the left side, a measure of tissue structure changed in step with how many hours of intervention a child received, and tracked improvements in their reading scores.

So the individual studies are not empty. The problem is that they do not agree. As the authors put it, the studies “do not converge on a specific set of functional brain changes associated with reading intervention.” Each one finds something. No two find quite the same thing. The authors attribute much of this to methodological heterogeneity, a plain way of saying the studies were built too differently to be compared cleanly. They used different tasks, different scanners, different contrasts, and often small samples. Several had problems with participants dropping out, and the authors flag an overall risk of bias in the literature.

Their conclusion is not that intervention fails to change the brain. It is that the field cannot yet say, with confidence, what that change looks like. They suggest the honest picture may be a shifting, network-wide one rather than a single spot that lights up.

What it means for you and your child

The first thing to take from this is reassurance, not doubt. Nothing here undermines reading intervention itself. A great deal of separate, robust evidence shows that structured, phonics-based teaching helps children with dyslexia read better. This review does not touch that. It is asking a narrower and more technical question: can we see a consistent fingerprint of that improvement on a brain scan? On that question alone, the answer so far is no.

That distinction matters because of how brain-imaging claims tend to reach parents. You may encounter a program, a clinic, or a device that promises to “rewire” or “retrain” your child’s brain, often illustrated with a colourful scan. This review is a useful corrective. The genuine researchers who have spent years pooling these scans cannot yet agree on what a rewired reading brain looks like. Any product that speaks with more certainty than that is selling confidence the science has not earned.

It is worth being clear about what a review like this can and cannot establish. It combines existing studies, so it inherits their limits. A null result in a meta-analysis is not proof that nothing changes. It can equally mean the studies were too small, too varied, or too few to detect a real effect that is there. Eight studies is not many. So this is best read as a map of where the evidence currently stands, not a verdict on the brain. The right response is patience, not disappointment.

There is also a practical lesson in how you judge whether an intervention is working. The signal you can actually trust is the one you can see yourself: is your child reading more accurately, more fluently, with less exhaustion at the end of a page? Those behavioral gains are what the strong evidence rests on, and they are what a scan, at best, is only trying to approximate. If a provider points to a brain image rather than to your child’s reading as proof of progress, ask to see the reading.

None of this should dim the encouragement at the heart of the matter. The children in these studies did change. Their reading improved, and their brains moved with it, in connections and in wiring, even if the exact location refused to hold still from one study to the next. The plasticity is real. Our ability to photograph it precisely is still catching up. For a parent, the sensible path is unchanged: seek out well-structured, evidence-based reading instruction, deliver it consistently, and measure success by the reading itself. The brain will do its work whether or not we have yet learned to see it clearly.

Reading intervention and neuroplasticity: A systematic review and meta-analysis of brain changes associated with reading intervention. Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews. 2022;132:465-494. doi:10.1016/j.neubiorev.2021.11.011 (PMC10327490). This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
