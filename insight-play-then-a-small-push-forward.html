Play, Then a Small Push Forward

A pilot trial of active group play in preschoolers found modest gains across all three parts of executive function. The signal is genuine but small, and the study is honest about how much it cannot yet settle.

Executive Function · Oxford Tutors · 6 minute read

What the research found

Executive function is the set of mental controls that let a child hold a goal in mind, resist a first impulse, and switch between tasks. Researchers usually break it into three parts: inhibitory control, working memory, and shifting. This study asked a narrow question. Does eight weeks of active group play, at a pace that gets four- and five-year-olds properly moving, improve those three controls more than an ordinary preschool routine does?

The trial was small and deliberately labelled a pilot. Sixty-two Chinese preschoolers took part, thirty in the intervention group and thirty-two in the control group. The intervention children did three sessions a week of moderate-to-vigorous group play, each lasting about fifty minutes, for eight weeks. The control children carried on as usual. Both groups were tested before, midway, and after. It is worth noting from the outset that the two groups were not well matched: the intervention children were younger on average (4.16 years against 4.7) and had fewer boys. The authors flag this themselves.

The direction of the results was consistent. On inhibitory control, measured by a task where children must give the opposite response to a silly sound, the intervention group rose from 16.87 to 19.43, while the control group moved from 15.78 to 17.19. On working memory, tested by remembering the contents of an “empty house,” the intervention group went from 10.43 to 13.47, against the control group’s 9.53 to 11.53. On shifting, measured by a card-sorting task where the rule changes, the intervention group climbed from 15.47 to 18.10, while the control group edged up from 14.94 to 15.91.

Read those numbers together and a pattern appears. Both groups improved on everything, which is what you expect from young children who are simply growing and attending preschool. The intervention children improved a little more on all three. The largest relative gap was in shifting, where the control group barely moved. Motor skills were also measured across six tests of running, jumping, throwing, flexibility, and balance. Both groups got better, and the intervention group somewhat more so.

What the study does not establish is as important as what it does. This is one small pilot, not a definitive answer. The sample is tiny, the groups differed in age and sex before the trial began, and there was no independent check on the children’s starting cognitive development. The authors are candid that their measurement tasks are imperfect, that each task measures more than the single skill it is meant to isolate, and that they used only a limited set of executive-function tests. A pilot is designed to show whether a larger, better-controlled study is worth running. On that modest question, the answer here is a cautious yes.

What it means for you and your child

The honest headline is that active group play appears to help preschoolers’ self-control and memory a little, on top of the substantial gains that come simply from being four or five and going to preschool. That is a reasonable thing to believe. It is not a reason to expect a transformation, and any product or program that promises one is going beyond this evidence.

Keep the size of the effect in view. The control children, doing nothing special, improved on every measure. Growth at this age is fast, and ordinary preschool life already does much of the work. The extra push from structured active play was real in this study but modest, and it came from a small, imperfectly matched sample. A single pilot cannot tell you how large the true benefit is, how long it lasts, or whether it would hold up in a bigger and more carefully balanced trial. Treat the finding as encouraging, not settled.

There is a practical takeaway that does not depend on the fine detail. Vigorous, playful physical activity is good for young children for many well-established reasons, and this study adds a plausible cognitive one. If your child is running, jumping, throwing, and taking turns in group games several times a week, that is likely to support the very controls that later help with sitting still, following instructions, and managing frustration. You do not need a branded curriculum to provide it. You need space, other children, and games with rules that change.

Be wary of the leap from “helps executive function in a trial” to “will raise school readiness” or “will improve grades.” This study did not measure school outcomes, and it did not follow the children over time. Executive function matters for learning, but the chain from an eight-week play program to a measurable academic result is long and untested here. The sensible reading is narrow and useful: movement-rich group play is worth building into a young child’s week, both for its own sake and for a modest, plausible cognitive bonus.

Finally, a note on how to read studies like this one at all. The strength of this paper is its honesty. The authors do not oversell. They list their limitations plainly, call the work a pilot, and stop short of grand claims. That restraint is a good sign in research, and a good model for how to hold the finding: a small, real, early result that points towards a larger question still worth asking.

Drawn from: Bai J, Huang H, Ouyang H. Effects of Group-Play Moderate to Vigorous Intensity Physical Activity Intervention on Executive Function and Motor Skills in 4- to 5-Year-Old Preschoolers: A Pilot Cluster Randomized Controlled Trial. Frontiers in Psychology. 2022;13:847785. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2022.847785 This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
