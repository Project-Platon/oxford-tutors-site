Nicolas Vivas Nikonorow

English Reading & Writing Educator · Literature, Composition & Great Books

A Yale-educated reading and writing mentor who co-founded the Oxford Tutors Great Books program.

Biography

Nicolas is a passionate English reading and writing educator dedicated to guiding students through challenging literature and helping them express their critical thinking and creative ideas on the page. Raised in a trilingual household in New York City, he developed a deep appreciation for the culture contained in language: the evocative power of an idiom and “the great issues that may hang from a bootlace.”

Upon completing his Bachelor of Arts in English at Yale University, Nicolas worked for five years in the publishing industry, including roles at ICM Partners and the Yale University Press. He transitioned to working as a full-time tutor eight years ago, initially supporting students with their college application essays before shifting his focus to reading comprehension and writing.

In 2026, he co-founded the Oxford Tutors Great Books summer program, intended to challenge high-achieving middle school and high school students with a diverse repertoire of summer reading and writing.

Nicolas plays classical cello in his free time and is pursuing a Master’s degree in musicology at the University of Warsaw.

Teaching philosophy

I am known for my patient, encouraging style and my ability to build students’ confidence, creating a supportive environment where they feel empowered to express themselves fully.

I love when my students are excited about what they’re reading and believe it’s important to build the habits that help students cultivate their bookshelves on their own. Library visits, blurb-reading, and first-chapter skimming are often part of the homework, and I am always eager to deviate from my curriculum when students bring their own book ideas to class. (I discovered many new favorites this way.) In addition to discussion-based analysis, close reading, and historical context, I like to incorporate memorization and verbal presentations to challenge students into using different neural pathways as they internalize their reading.

Learning to write is learning to think. It’s learning to organize your thoughts on a page and hone the logic that weaves them together. As with any craft, it requires regular practice, and students must be ready to put in the work. Lessons typically begin with the foundations, targeted grammar practice, vocabulary, sentence and paragraph structure, and move up to elements of style and broader essay form. Despite the many challenges, there’s nothing quite like the satisfaction of finding a perfect turn of phrase or hearing a paragraph finally ring true. As a firm believer in the pedagogical merits of silliness, I also enjoy using creative writing exercises to fight writer’s block and transform the blank page into a space for fun and adventure.

I meet each student at their unique skill level and help them develop the tools that allow them to grow on their own. Lessons are not a time for powering through a week’s worth of assignments but rather a check-in point to review specific questions and revisions, discuss and analyze tricky passages, celebrate students’ hard work, and establish a plan for the coming week. I am happiest when I feel my students taking the reins: developing their own instincts for revision, delighting over an old classic or bestseller, and becoming accomplices in bookish mischief.
