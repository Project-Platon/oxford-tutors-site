Thirty-Five Years On, Acceleration Did Not Harm Them

Three cohorts of gifted children were tracked for over 35 years. By age 50, how much they had been accelerated in school bore no relationship to their psychological well-being.

Giftedness · Oxford Tutors · 6 minute read

What the research found

One of the most persistent worries about gifted education is that moving a child ahead of their age group, whether by skipping a grade or entering university early, might exact a hidden social or emotional cost. The child gains academically, the thinking goes, but pays later in loneliness or unease. This study set out to test that worry against the longest kind of evidence there is: a life measured across decades.

The researchers drew on gifted young people identified before the age of 13 and then followed for more than 35 years, checking in with them at age 50. There were three cohorts, defined by how rare their early ability was. Cohort 1, identified in 1972 to 1974, comprised 1,020 students in the top 1 percent of ability. Cohort 2, identified in 1976 to 1979, comprised 396 students in the top 0.5 percent. Cohort 3, identified from 1980 to 1983, comprised 220 students in the top 0.01 percent, a group the authors call profoundly gifted. A second study added 478 elite graduate students in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics, identified in 1992 and followed from age 25 to age 50.

The question was simple to state. Among these gifted individuals, did the amount of academic acceleration they experienced in youth predict how they were faring psychologically at 50? The outcomes were several well-established indicators of psychological well-being, assessed decades after the acceleration itself.

The answer was equally clear. The amount of acceleration did not covary with psychological well-being in either study. Those who had been accelerated more were, on the whole, neither better nor worse off in midlife than those who had been accelerated less. And there was a second finding worth holding onto: the well-being of these participants, taken as a group, sat above the average of national comparison samples. These were not fragile people. If anything, they were doing well.

The authors' conclusion is direct. Concerns about the long-term social and emotional effects of acceleration for high-potential students, they write, appear to be unwarranted.

It is worth being precise about what this design can and cannot show. This is a longitudinal, observational study, not an experiment. No child was randomly assigned to be accelerated. Families and schools chose acceleration for particular children in particular circumstances, and those children may differ in ways the study cannot fully separate from acceleration itself. The absence of a relationship between acceleration and well-being is reassuring, but it is not the same as proving that acceleration causes good outcomes. The cohorts were also identified in the 1970s and 1980s and are unusually able by any measure, so their experience may not transfer neatly to every gifted child or every school system today.

What it means for you and your child

For a parent weighing whether to let a child move ahead, the fear is rarely about the academics. It is the quieter question: will she be lonely, will he miss something, will they look back and wish they had simply been allowed to be their age? This study speaks directly to that fear, and its message is calming. Across an unusually long horizon, more acceleration did not track with worse psychological well-being. The children who were moved ahead were not, decades later, paying a hidden price.

That is a genuinely useful thing to know, because the worry is so common and so rarely tested over a real lifetime. Much of what is written about gifted children's social and emotional lives leans on snapshots taken in adolescence. Here we have people at 50, looking back on a whole arc, and the arc does not bend the way the anxiety predicts.

Still, we would read this as permission rather than prescription. The study says acceleration did not harm these children on average; it does not say acceleration is right for yours. A decision to skip a grade or start university early is a decision about one specific child, in one specific school, with one specific set of friendships and interests. The value of this research is that it removes a particular fear from the scales, so that the real questions, about fit, readiness, and what the child wants, can be weighed on their own terms.

We would offer the same caution we always do about a single study, however long and however careful. This is one body of work, following a very able group of people identified decades ago. It maps a relationship, or rather the absence of one, and it does so persuasively. Read alongside the wider literature on acceleration, which has tended to reach similar conclusions, it adds a reassuring and hard-won data point. On its own, it is a reason to set aside a common worry, not a reason to accelerate any particular child.

Drawn from: Bernstein, B. O., Lubinski, D., & Benbow, C. P. (2021). Academic Acceleration in Gifted Youth and Fruitless Concerns Regarding Psychological Well-Being: A 35-Year Longitudinal Study. Journal of Educational Psychology, 113(4), 830-845. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35937558/ This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
