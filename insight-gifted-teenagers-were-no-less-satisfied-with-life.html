Gifted Teenagers Were No Less Satisfied With Life

A German study of 655 high-school students compared the life satisfaction of gifted and non-gifted teenagers. The gap the popular image predicts was not there.

Giftedness · Oxford Tutors · 5 minute read

What the research found

There is a familiar picture of the gifted adolescent: bright but burdened, out of step with peers, quietly unhappy. It is a durable image, and it shapes how many parents and teachers approach a very able child. This study asked whether the image holds up when you actually measure how satisfied gifted teenagers are with their lives, and compare them fairly with everyone else.

The researchers studied a sample of 655 German high-school students, of whom 75 met a clear and demanding definition of giftedness: general intelligence more than two standard deviations above the mean, which places a student in roughly the top two percent. This matters, because much earlier research on gifted well-being drew its conclusions from students already enrolled in special programs, who are not representative of gifted young people in general. Here the sample was relatively unselected, and the comparison groups of non-gifted students were chosen to make the contrast a fair one.

The central result was an absence. The difference in life satisfaction between gifted and non-gifted adolescents was smaller than a tenth of a standard deviation, a gap so slight it is fair to call it no difference at all. Gifted teenagers, on this measure, were living lives about as satisfying as their peers.

The study did find one difference, but it was not about giftedness. Girls reported somewhat lower life satisfaction than boys, by about a quarter of a standard deviation. Crucially, this pattern ran across the whole sample rather than being concentrated among the gifted. Gifted girls, the authors note specifically, were not found to be especially dissatisfied with their lives. The gender pattern is a feature of adolescence broadly, not a special affliction of the gifted.

It is worth being precise about what this design can and cannot show. This is a cross-sectional study, a snapshot rather than a film. It captures how satisfied these students were at one moment, not how their satisfaction rose or fell over time, and it cannot rule out that particular gifted children face particular struggles that a group average smooths over. Life satisfaction is also a broad, self-reported measure; it does not speak to every dimension of a young person's inner life. And the sample is German high-school students, so the findings belong to that setting.

What it means for you and your child

The most useful thing this study does is loosen the grip of a stereotype. If you have a gifted child, you have almost certainly encountered the assumption, sometimes voiced, often just implied, that unusual ability comes bundled with unusual unhappiness. This research suggests that, as a general rule, it does not. On the whole, gifted teenagers report being about as satisfied with their lives as anyone else their age.

That should ease a background worry, but it should not switch off attention. A group average is a statement about the group, not a promise about the individual in front of you. Some gifted children are genuinely lonely, or anxious, or bored in ways that matter, and the fact that most of their peers are content does not lessen what they are feeling. The study frees you from assuming that giftedness itself is the source of unhappiness; it does not free you from noticing when your own child is struggling.

There is a quieter, practical point in the gender finding too. The dip in girls' life satisfaction was not about being gifted; it was part of a wider pattern across adolescence. For a parent of a bright teenage girl, that is a reminder to look at the ordinary pressures of being a teenager, and not to file every difficulty under the heading of giftedness. Sometimes the more powerful explanation is the more ordinary one.

We would offer the same caution we always do about a single study, however well constructed. This is one careful snapshot of one national sample at one point in time. It does a good job of correcting a common overstatement, and its methods are a clear improvement on much of what came before. Read alongside the wider literature, it belongs to a growing body of work suggesting that giftedness, on its own, is not a risk factor for a diminished emotional life. On its own, it is a reason to hold the stereotype lightly, and to keep watching the actual child.

Drawn from: Bergold, S., Wirthwein, L., Rost, D. H., & Steinmayr, R. (2015). Are gifted adolescents more satisfied with their lives than their non-gifted peers? Frontiers in Psychology, 6, 1623. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26539152/ This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
