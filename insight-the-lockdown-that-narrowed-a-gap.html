The Lockdown That Narrowed a Gap

A Dutch study of 312 families found the COVID-19 school lockdown hurt all primary children, but hit non-gifted children harder, briefly narrowing a gap that had favored them.

Giftedness · Oxford Tutors · 5 minute read

What the research found

The COVID-19 school closures were an unwelcome natural experiment. Overnight, the structure of school was stripped away, and children were left to learn at home. This study used that moment to ask a specific question: did the lockdown affect gifted primary-school children differently from their peers?

The researchers surveyed the parents of 312 primary-school children in the Netherlands, 122 of them parents of gifted children. Working within a well-established framework called self-determination theory, they looked at three things before and during the lockdown: the satisfaction of children's basic psychological needs, namely their sense of autonomy, competence, and relatedness; their well-being; and their motivation.

The broad picture was, unsurprisingly, negative. Across the board, the lockdown pushed all three measures down, for gifted and non-gifted children alike. Home learning, for most children, meant less need satisfaction, lower well-being, and weaker motivation. So far, so grim, and so expected.

The more surprising finding was a levelling. Before the lockdown, the gifted children in this sample actually reported lower need satisfaction, well-being, and motivation than their non-gifted peers. During the lockdown, that gap narrowed, not because the gifted children improved, but because the non-gifted children fell further. The declines were steeper for the non-gifted group. The study also traced different routes for the two groups: for non-gifted children, the drops in well-being and motivation ran through losses in autonomy and in relatedness with classmates, while for gifted children, changes were carried by their sense of competence.

It is worth being precise about what this design can and cannot show. The before-and-during measures were gathered from parents, reporting on their children, which is a step removed from the children's own accounts. The comparison is between two time points around an extraordinary event, so we cannot cleanly separate the lockdown itself from everything else changing in that period. And this is one Dutch sample of primary-age children during a unique moment, so its lessons may not generalize to other ages, places, or ordinary times.

What it means for you and your child

The detail most worth carrying away is the one about what carried the harm, because it differed by child. For the non-gifted children, the damage came largely through lost autonomy and lost connection with classmates, the freedom and the friendships that school ordinarily supplies. For the gifted children, the thread that mattered most was competence, the sense of being good at something and being suitably challenged.

That distinction is a practical guide, not just a research curiosity. It suggests that when a gifted child's motivation or mood dips, whether in a lockdown or in any disrupted stretch, the lever most likely to help is a renewed sense of competence: meaningful challenge, work that stretches without overwhelming, the felt experience of getting good at something hard. For many other children, the more powerful levers are connection and a degree of self-direction. One size does not fit.

There is also a quiet corrective in the finding that, before the disruption, the gifted children were faring slightly worse on these measures than their peers. It is a reminder that giftedness is not a guarantee of a smooth ride, and that an able child's needs for challenge and competence can go unmet even in ordinary times. The lockdown did not create that vulnerability; it only rearranged who was struggling most.

We would offer the same caution we always do about a single study, and more so given its unusual context. This is one parent-reported snapshot of a singular event in one country. It should not be read as a general law about gifted children and adversity. Its usefulness is in the mechanism it points to, that competence matters especially for gifted children's motivation. On its own, it is a reason to attend to challenge and mastery when a gifted child falters, not a blueprint for any particular disruption.

Drawn from: Sam-Sen Bronsveld, S., et al. (2022). Impact of the COVID-19 lockdown on gifted and non-gifted primary school students' need satisfaction, well-being, and motivation. High Ability Studies / Frontiers, 2022. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36718255/ This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
