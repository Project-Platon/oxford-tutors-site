When a Gifted Teenager Tries Alcohol

A study of seventh graders found gifted students tried alcohol at about the same rate as their peers, but the gifted ones who did seemed better adjusted, not worse.

Giftedness · Oxford Tutors · 5 minute read

What the research found

There are two opposite stories parents tell themselves about gifted teenagers and risk. In one, the bright child is too sensible to experiment, insulated by good judgment. In the other, the bright child is bored and precocious, and finds trouble early. This study looked at one concrete behavior, trying alcohol in early adolescence, and found that neither story is quite right.

The researchers studied seventh graders, a group of more than 300 students of whom just under half, about 42.5 percent, were identified as academically gifted. Early adolescence is a sensible place to look, because it is when many children first encounter alcohol, and the study asked a simple question to begin with: did gifted students try it at different rates than their classmates? The more interesting question came second: among the students who had tried alcohol, did giftedness change what that behavior was connected to?

The first answer was a flat one. Gifted students tried alcohol at rates that did not differ from their non-gifted peers. Whatever protective aura we might imagine around a very able child, it did not show up here as abstinence. Bright seventh graders were about as likely to have tried a drink as anyone else.

The second answer was the more telling. Giftedness acted as a moderator: gifted students who had tried alcohol looked less at risk than non-gifted students who had done the same. Across several markers of adjustment, including overt aggression, delinquency, and the deviancy of their friends, the gifted drinkers were, on the whole, better adjusted than their non-gifted counterparts. The same act carried a different profile depending on the child. The authors add a note of realism, though: gifted young people who use alcohol may drift toward peer groups where substances are welcome, and that association could store up risk for later.

It is worth being precise about what this design can and cannot show. This is a cross-sectional study, capturing a single moment in early adolescence. It cannot follow these children forward to see whether the better-adjusted gifted drinkers stayed that way, or whether the drift toward substance-friendly peers eventually caught up with them. Trying alcohol was measured simply, as a yes-or-no, which says nothing about how much or how often. And the study describes one cohort of seventh graders, not gifted teenagers everywhere.

What it means for you and your child

The first thing to take from this is a small deflation of a comforting myth. Being gifted did not keep these children from trying alcohol. If part of you has assumed that a clever, high-achieving child will simply opt out of ordinary adolescent experimentation, this is a gentle correction. Ability and curiosity are not the same as caution, and the pressures of early adolescence reach able children too.

The second thing is more reassuring, and worth holding with care. When gifted children in this study had tried alcohol, it was less entangled with the troubling company that the same behavior kept for others, less aggression, less delinquency, less deviant friendship. For a parent, that suggests a drink tried at twelve or thirteen may not mean the same thing for a well-adjusted gifted child as the headlines about teenage drinking imply. It is a reason not to catastrophise a single episode.

But the authors' own caveat is the part to keep. They noticed that gifted drinkers can gravitate toward peer settings where substances are normal, and that is where a present-tense reassurance can quietly become a future-tense problem. The practical reading is neither alarm nor complacency. It is to pay less attention to the isolated fact of a first drink and more to the company and the pattern, the friendships forming, the settings a child is drawn to, the direction of travel.

We would offer the same caution we always do about a single study, however interesting. This is one snapshot of one group of seventh graders, and it measures a first sip rather than a habit. It should not be read as permission, nor as a promise that gifted children are safe. Read alongside the wider literature on adolescent risk, it makes a narrow, useful point: the same behavior can mean different things in different children. On its own, it is a reason to watch the context rather than panic at the act.

Drawn from: Peairs, K. F., Eichen, D., Putallaz, M., Costanzo, P. R., & Grimes, C. L. (2011). Academic Giftedness and Alcohol Use in Early Adolescence. Gifted Child Quarterly, 55(2), 95-110. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21949444/ This essay is written for families; the paper itself is the fuller, technical account.
